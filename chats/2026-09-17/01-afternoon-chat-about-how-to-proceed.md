Yes. I think we've now got enough material that I can stop treating this as a file-access experiment and actually **pick up the project**.

 And the extra point you've just made is important: **the companion is a standalone Fallout 4 mod. ATWB is its backstory/source material, not a prerequisite.** A player who has never heard Tommi's show should still understand Westminster's story. The player who _has_ played ATWB gets an additional layer of recognition and emotional resonance. That's a significant design constraint, and I'll treat it as such.

 ## What I think we've actually been building

 There are really **three layers**, rather than simply "a sequel to ATWB".

 ### 1\. _As the World Burns_ is Tommi's story

 ATWB is essentially a radio drama disguised as a Fallout radio station.

 Tommi Starr is a 2077 London pirate-radio DJ, broadcasting C-ROK's _As the World Burns_. His material is deliberately mundane: music, beer, his cat, his flatmate, Bob, his sister Lois, his landlord, coffee, parties, sex, clothes, annoying people, his increasingly alarming observations about the world.

 And that's precisely why it works.

 He isn't sitting there explaining the Fallout timeline to the player. He's **a bloke living his life** while the world is approaching nuclear annihilation. The historical information emerges incidentally because that's what happens when somebody talks about their life.  GitHub+1

 The actual script strongly reinforces this. Tommi can spend several minutes discussing the Chinese-American war, stealth submarines and powered armour, then immediately worry about his cat, complain about pockets, get drunk, or discover that his best friend Bob is marrying his estranged sister.  GitHub+2

 And the final broadcasts contain the thing that I think is most important to carry into the companion:

 > the world is ending, but **life doesn't stop being life**.

 Tommi's philosophy eventually crystallises into something like _the world is fucked; try not to make it worse_. He isn't a saint or a prophet. He's funny, selfish, horny, irresponsible, depressed, kind, ridiculous and occasionally surprisingly thoughtful.  GitHub+1

 That's what makes him worth inheriting.

---

 ## 2\. Westminster's story is not "ATWB 2"

 This is the distinction I think you've just clarified particularly well.

 Westminster **doesn't need to know who Tommi is**.

 The player doesn't need to know who Tommi is.

 The companion needs to work if someone installs it having never played ATWB.

 The story begins with Westminster's own problem:

 **Something isn't right.**

 He's forty years old in 2287. This isn't a young person's "what am I going to do with my life?" crisis. He's already lived forty years. He's got a life, a history and an identity that he presumably thinks he understands.

 But something about himself doesn't sit correctly.

 He doesn't initially think:

 > I am going to investigate my great-great-great-grandfather.

 He thinks:

 > **What the hell is wrong with me?**

 And because this is Fallout, there are _lots_ of plausible answers.

 Maybe he's genetically unusual. Maybe it's neurological. Maybe it's radiation. Maybe it's Vault 81. Maybe it's some inherited condition. Maybe there's something in his family history. Maybe somebody knows something. The story deliberately lets those possibilities feel credible.  GitHub+1

 That's what gets him moving.

---

 ## 3\. The genealogy is the mechanism, not the answer

 This is probably the most important structural thing we've arrived at.

 Westminster follows the chain backwards:

 **Westminster → E → D → C → B → A → Abbey → Tommi → 2077**

 The fascinating thing is that this looks like a conventional genealogy mystery.

 But structurally it is doing something else.

 He's asking:

 > **Where did I come from?**

 because he believes that if he can establish the cause of himself, he'll finally understand himself.

 And gradually the story demonstrates that those aren't the same question.

 His ancestors can explain his history.

 His medical records can tell him things about his body.

 Vault 81 can explain some things.

 Far Harbor can give him another way of thinking about identity.

 Nuka-World can give him another plausible explanation.

 Mystics, doctors, herbalists and fortune tellers can all provide fragments.

 But none of them can answer:

 > **Who am I?**

 That is where the genealogy becomes metaphor rather than merely plot.  GitHub+1

 # And I think I understand the role of Tommi now

 This is where the ATWB source material becomes really valuable.

 Tommi shouldn't be a prerequisite for understanding Westminster.

 He should be a **reward for discovering Westminster's history**.

 At first the family tree is just names.

 Then perhaps Westminster discovers Abbey.

 Then A, B, C, D and E become actual people.

 And eventually he discovers Tommi.

 And suddenly one of those dry genealogical names **starts talking**.

 That's the emotional trick.

 The player doesn't need to know Tommi beforehand. In fact, I think it may be better if they don't. Westminster can encounter him exactly as the player does:

 > _Who the fuck is this guy?_

 And then:

 > _Oh. This is my ancestor._

 And then, more importantly:

 > _Oh shit. He's a person._

 The radio recording is suddenly no longer "lore". It's a surviving human being.

 The writers' bible calls Tommi effectively a "ghost" without needing him literally to be one: Westminster expects history and instead encounters a personality.  GitHub

 That's enormously useful for the standalone requirement, because **the companion can explain Tommi through Westminster's discovery of him**. You don't need prior knowledge.

 # The ATWB player gets a second layer

 This is the part I think is worth deliberately designing for.

 Someone who hasn't played ATWB hears:

 > "Here's a recording made by my ancestor, Tommi Starr."

 They discover Tommi.

 They hear this weird, funny, messy guy talking about 2077.

 They gradually understand who he was.

 That's a complete experience.

 But someone who _has_ played ATWB hears Tommi's voice and thinks:

 > **Oh my God. I know this guy.**

 They remember the songs.

 They remember Bob.

 They remember Lois.

 They remember the stupid jokes.

 They remember Tommi talking about the world getting worse.

 And perhaps they remember things Westminster doesn't yet know.

 That creates a wonderful asymmetry between **player knowledge and character knowledge**.

 But it must never become:

 > "You should have played my other mod."

 It should be:

 > "If you played my other mod, you just got punched in the feelings."

 That's a much better relationship between the two projects.

 # The investigation needs to be genuine

 One thing I don't want to lose from the previous work is the rule that the false explanations shouldn't be _stupid_.

 The doctors aren't necessarily idiots.

 The mystic isn't necessarily a fraud.

 The Vault 81 evidence shouldn't necessarily be fabricated.

 Something genuinely strange may be present.

 Something genetic may genuinely be present.

 Something in Westminster's family history may genuinely be important.

 Far Harbor may genuinely tell him something profound.

 Nuka-World may genuinely contain useful evidence.

 The problem is that Westminster keeps making the same logical mistake:

 > **This explains something about me, therefore this explains me.**

 The evidence can be true while the conclusion is wrong.  GitHub+1

 That gives us much richer quest design than a succession of "LOL, that guy was wrong" encounters.

 # And then comes the turn

 Eventually the mystic essentially says:

 > **No. It's none of that.**

 You've been looking outward.

 You've investigated genes.

 Disease.

 Radiation.

 Vault 81.

 Ancestors.

 History.

 Everything except yourself.

 And the really good version of this isn't the mystic saying:

 > "You are X."

 Because that would just make the mystic another person **telling Westminster who he is**.

 Instead:

 > **You already know.**

 And:

 > **Once you understand it, go to XXX.**

 That's the hand-off from investigation to agency.  GitHub+1

 The mystic doesn't give Westminster an identity.

 They give him permission to stop looking for one somewhere else.

 # The trans element

 I have this clearly in the material, and your framing is important.

 If Westminster is trans, the story cannot ultimately pull the rug out and say:

 > "Aha! The thing you've been investigating was actually your biological transgender mutation."

 That would retrospectively make his identity the disease the mystery was designed to uncover.

 The useful distinction established in the earlier work is between:

 > **Something is wrong with me**

 and

 > **There is something about myself I don't yet understand.**

 His discomfort is real.

 His search is real.

 His explanations are wrong or incomplete.

 His eventual recognition is real.

 But **being trans isn't the plot's medical diagnosis**. The revelation is recognition rather than pathology.  GitHub+1

 And importantly, he doesn't necessarily emerge with everything perfectly sorted out. The important thing is that he finally understands what direction he needs to move in.  GitHub

 # The ending is where the whole thing has to pay off

 This is the bit we hadn't solved.

 **XXX remains genuinely open.**

 And I think that's good.

 Because we've established a very strong structural progression:

 **Act I:** Something is wrong.

 **Act II:** Something happened before me.

 **Act III:** Everything means something.

 **Act IV:** I'm asking the wrong question.

 **Act V:** Recognition.

 **Act VI:** **Now do something.**

 The story has spent almost its entire duration going **backwards**.

 Westminster follows his ancestry backwards.

 He follows records backwards.

 He follows Tommi backwards.

 He follows history backwards.

 Eventually he has to **turn around**.

 That's why XXX cannot simply be another location where somebody hands him another piece of lore.

 It has to be somewhere he goes **because he now knows who he is**.

 And then he has to _do something_.  GitHub+1

 That's the difference between the mystery's resolution and the story's resolution.

 ## And ATWB's title suddenly applies to both stories

 I think this is one of the strongest things in the whole concept.

 Tommi:

 > **The world is fucked. Anyway, here's some music.**

 Westminster begins:

 > **Something is wrong with me. I need to find out what.**

 And eventually:

 > **The world is fucked. I know who I am. Now what am I going to do?**

 Tommi can't stop the apocalypse.

 Westminster can't change his ancestry.

 Neither can change the world they inherited.

 But Westminster can choose what he does with himself.

 That's the inheritance Tommi ultimately passes on—not his DNA, not his records, not his music collection, but **the idea that when the world is terrible, you still get to decide what sort of person you're going to be in it.**  GitHub

 And there's a lovely detail in the actual ATWB script that makes this more than something we invented afterwards: near the end, Tommi explicitly talks about judging his day by whether he made anybody else's life worse, and about trying to do slightly more than the bare minimum.  GitHub

 So I now see the companion as **not really being about Tommi at all**.

 It's about Westminster.

 Tommi is the person who allows Westminster—and eventually the player—to discover that history isn't just dates and bloodlines. It's made of actual people who lived ordinary lives, loved people, made mistakes, worried about stupid things, and tried to get through the day.

 And then Westminster has to decide what to do with the fact that he has inherited all of that.

 **I think that's the project we've been circling.**

 And with the `.txt` now accessible, I can use the actual ATWB material rather than the earlier summaries whenever we need to work out whether something genuinely fits Tommi, his voice, his relationships, or the world he inhabited.  GitHub+1

 One thing I would _not_ do yet is try to turn all this into a new polished "bible". There are still enough deliberately unresolved pieces—especially **what Westminster actually experiences, how the genealogy unfolds, what the player does at each stage, and what XXX is**—that I think we should preserve the uncertainty while we develop those pieces.
